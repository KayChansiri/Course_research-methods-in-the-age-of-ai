## Course Description

Research methods are changing as computation, large language models, and increasingly accessible analytical tools become part of everyday research.

This course introduces graduate students to the logic and practice of mass communication research while integrating contemporary computational tools.

You will learn how to move from a research question to evidence: reviewing literature, selecting appropriate research designs, organizing and analyzing data, interpreting findings, and communicating results.

The course combines traditional research methodology with modern research tools, including Python, Google Colab, pretrained language models, and generative AI. Programming is treated as a research tool rather than as an end in itself. No prior programming experience is required.

**Tools can assist research, but they cannot replace methodological judgment.**

Throughout the course, you will learn not only how to use computational and AI tools, but also when they are appropriate, how to evaluate their outputs, how to identify limitations, and how to use them responsibly.

## Course Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, you should be able to:

### Overall Research Competency

1. Explain the major stages of the research process and connect research questions to evidence, research design, data analysis, interpretation, and communication of findings.

### Block 1 — Modern Research Tools in the Age of AI

2. Explain foundational concepts behind contemporary AI and large language models at a level relevant to communication researchers.
3. Use Google Colab and basic Python to support reproducible research tasks.
4. Critically evaluate the capabilities, limitations, and appropriate uses of AI and pretrained language models in research workflows.

### Block 2 — Data Collection and Research Design

5. Develop research questions and hypotheses grounded in scholarly literature.
6. Distinguish among major quantitative research designs, including surveys, experiments, A/B tests, and quasi-experiments.
7. Identify and justify appropriate sampling, measurement, and data-collection strategies.

### Block 3 — Data Analysis and Interpretation

8. Select and conduct introductory statistical analyses, including correlation, t-tests, regression, ANOVA, and chi-square.
9. Apply introductory computational approaches to text data, including sentiment analysis or text classification.
10. Interpret analytical results in substantive rather than purely statistical terms, recognizing relevant assumptions and limitations.

### Block 4 — Responsible Research and Communication

11. Identify ethical, validity, and reproducibility concerns associated with computational and AI-assisted research.
12. Communicate research findings and their limitations to academic, industry, and other stakeholder audiences.

## Course Structure

The course is organized into four interconnected blocks that guide students from foundational computational literacy to research design, data analysis, and responsible communication of findings.

Because AI and computational tools are increasingly integrated into research practice, the course begins by introducing these tools and their capabilities and limitations. This foundation prepares students to evaluate when and how to use them throughout the research process.

### Block 1 — Modern Research Tools in the Age of AI

**Weeks 1–5 | Computational Literacy and Critical Evaluation**

Students are introduced to how AI and computational tools are changing research practices.

Topics include:

- Research methods in the age of AI
- Large language models and Transformer foundations
- Python and Google Colab
- Pretrained language models
- Human evaluation of AI-assisted research workflows

Through guided activities, students compare human judgment with AI-assisted research tasks and learn to evaluate the capabilities, limitations, and appropriate uses of these tools.

The emphasis is on **computational literacy and critical evaluation**, not software engineering or memorizing programming syntax.

This foundation prepares students to integrate computational tools thoughtfully into subsequent research activities.

### Block 2 — Data Collection and Research Design

**Weeks 6–8 | Methodological Decisions and Research Validity**

Building on this foundation, students turn to the methodological decisions that determine the quality of research.

Topics include:

- Research questions and hypotheses
- Scholarly literature and research rationale
- Sampling and measurement
- Surveys
- Randomized experiments and A/B testing
- Quasi-experimental designs
- Reliability and validity

Students learn to select and justify research designs based on their research questions, recognizing that computational and AI tools can support—but cannot replace—sound methodological judgment.

### Block 3 — Data Analysis and Interpretation

**Weeks 9–15 | Statistical Reasoning and Computational Analysis**

Students apply their computational foundation and methodological knowledge to preparing, analyzing, and interpreting research data.

Topics include:

- Data structure and preparation
- Data cleaning and descriptive statistics
- Correlation and t-tests
- Linear regression
- Analysis of variance (ANOVA)
- Chi-square tests
- Computational text analysis and sentiment classification

Most methods follow a **concept → guided application** structure using Python and Google Colab.

The emphasis is on selecting appropriate analytical methods, interpreting findings, evaluating assumptions, and understanding limitations rather than extensive programming.

### Block 4 — Responsible Research and Communication

**Week 16 | Research Ethics and Stakeholder Communication**

The final block brings together the knowledge and skills developed throughout the course.

Students consider:

- Research ethics and responsible AI use
- Research validity and limitations
- Reproducibility
- Human oversight of computational tools
- Interpretation and communication of research findings
- Communicating evidence to academic, industry, and other stakeholder audiences

Through their final projects, students demonstrate how research questions, methodological decisions, analytical results, and responsible communication form an integrated research process.

## Technology and Computational Learning

The course integrates several computational and AI tools to support applied research activities.

You will work with:

- **Google Colab** for browser-based computational notebooks
- **Python** for introductory data management and analysis
- **pandas and NumPy** for working with structured data
- **SciPy and statsmodels** for introductory statistical analyses
- **Matplotlib** for data visualization
- **Pretrained language models** for guided computational text activities
- **Generative AI** for selected research and coding activities
- **GitHub** for instructional materials and computational resources

No prior programming experience is required.

You are not expected to memorize Python syntax or become a software engineer.

The emphasis is on understanding **what computational tools are doing, when they are appropriate, and how their outputs should be evaluated**.

### Laboratories and Follow-Along Notebooks

Some class sessions include guided laboratory activities, while others use Google Colab notebooks to support demonstrations and instructional examples.

**Not every week includes a formal laboratory.**

A link to a Colab notebook does not necessarily indicate a laboratory session or an online class. Many notebooks are provided so students can follow along with examples during regular in-person instruction or review the analytical workflow afterward.

JOUR 701 is an in-person course. Selected computational laboratory sessions are conducted synchronously through Zoom to facilitate screen sharing, collaborative coding, troubleshooting, and group activities.

Students should refer to the official syllabus and Blackboard announcements for the designated Zoom laboratory dates and the most current meeting information.

## AI Use and Human Oversight

AI is intentionally incorporated into selected course activities.

Students will learn to use AI systems as **research tools whose outputs require evaluation**, rather than as substitutes for independent research judgment.

Depending on the activity, AI may support:

- Literature organization
- Classification tasks
- Coding assistance
- Debugging
- Computational research workflows
- Methodological critique

However, permission to use AI depends on the assignment.

Students remain responsible for:

- Verifying AI-generated information and code
- Understanding the research methods and analyses they use
- Identifying potential errors, biases, and limitations
- Protecting confidential and sensitive information
- Acknowledging AI use when required
- Making independent methodological and interpretive decisions

AI may not be used during examinations or assessments unless explicitly permitted by the instructor.

Submitting AI-generated work as one's own independent work when such use has not been authorized may constitute an academic-integrity violation.

**Human judgment, methodological reasoning, and responsible evaluation remain central throughout the course.**
