    Course Resources

    Online Books and Course Readings

    This course does not rely on a single required textbook. Instead, readings are selected from openly available books, peer-reviewed scholarly articles, research-methods resources, and technical documentation.

    Readings are organized around the course's four instructional blocks, progressing from computational and AI literacy to research design, data analysis, and responsible research communication.

    Blackboard remains the official source for required readings, weekly preparation instructions, assignment details, and course updates. This page provides a consolidated reference to major course readings and additional resources for review and independent learning.

    You are not expected to read every resource listed here in full. Focus on the assigned chapters, sections, and concepts identified in Blackboard announcements.

    Block 1 — Modern Research Tools in the Age of AI

    Weeks 1–5 | Computational Literacy and AI-Assisted Research

    The first block introduces the computational environments and AI technologies increasingly used in contemporary research. Students learn foundational concepts behind large language models, Python, and pretrained language models while developing the ability to critically evaluate their use in research workflows.

    The goal is computational literacy and methodological judgment, not software engineering or memorizing programming syntax.

    Research Methods and Computational Social Science

    Edelmann, A., Wolff, T., Montagne, D., & Bail, C. A. (2020). Computational Social Science and Sociology.

    Annual Review of Sociology, 46, 61–81.

    Read the article

    This reading introduces how computational methods and digital data are changing social-science research.

    Focus on the introduction and the discussion of how computational approaches expand research opportunities while creating methodological challenges.

    Block 2 — Data Collection and Research Design

    Weeks 6–8 | Research Questions, Sampling, Measurement, and Research Design

    Building on the computational foundation introduced in Block 1, this block focuses on the methodological decisions that determine research quality.

    Students learn to formulate research questions and hypotheses, select appropriate research designs, operationalize variables, and evaluate sampling, measurement, and validity.

    The emphasis is on understanding why a particular research design is appropriate for a research question, rather than simply identifying different research methods.

    Research Questions, Sampling, and Measurement

    Sampling Methods in Mass Communication Research

    Erba et al. (2018). Sampling Methods and Sample Populations in Quantitative Mass Communication Research Studies: A 15-Year Census of Six Journals.

    Communication Research Reports, 35(1), 42–47.

    Read the article

    Focus on:

    • Probability versus nonprobability sampling
    • Sampling strategies in mass communication research
    • Representativeness
    • Sampling limitations and generalizability

    Measurement, Reliability, and Validity

    Mazer, J. P., & Graham, E. E. (2015). Measurement in Instructional Communication Research: A Decade in Review.

    Communication Education, 64(2).

    Read the article

    Focus on:

    • Conceptualization and operationalization
    • Measurement decisions
    • Reliability
    • Validity
    • The importance of selecting appropriate measures

    Research Design Review and Application

    The research design readings support the Research Design Lab and Assignment 4: Research Design Brief.

    Students should be able to explain:

    1. What research question or hypothesis is being investigated?
    2. Who or what is the population of interest, and how will the sample be selected?
    3. What are the major variables, and how will they be measured?
    4. Which research design is most appropriate, and why?
    5. What are the strengths, limitations, and potential validity concerns of the proposed design?

    The purpose of this block is to develop sound methodological reasoning before moving into statistical analysis.

    Block 3 — Data Analysis and Interpretation

    Weeks 9–15 | Data Preparation, Statistical Analysis, and Computational Text Analysis

    Students apply their computational and research-design knowledge to preparing, analyzing, and interpreting research data.

    The emphasis is on understanding the relationship between research questions, variable types, analytical methods, statistical outputs, and substantive interpretation.

    How to Approach Block 3 Readings

    You are not expected to memorize formulas or Python syntax.

    For each statistical method, focus on four questions:

    1. What kind of research question does this method answer?
    2. What types of variables does it use?
    3. What does the Python output tell us?
    4. How can the result be explained in plain language?

    The lectures and Google Colab notebooks provide guided practice with the analytical workflows students are expected to understand.

    Getting Technical Help

    Debugging is a normal part of computational research.

    When requesting technical help from the instructor, classmates, documentation, or an authorized AI coding assistant, try to provide:

    1. Your objective — What are you trying to accomplish?
    2. The relevant data — What variables or data structure are involved?
    3. Your code — What did you run?
    4. The error — What happened?
    5. Your expectation — What did you expect to happen?
    6. What you already tried — What changes or solutions have you tested?

    Providing this information makes it easier to diagnose problems and helps you develop a systematic, reproducible approach to computational problem-solving.

    When sharing code, error messages, or sample data, remove confidential or personally identifiable information.
